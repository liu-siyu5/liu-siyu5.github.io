Pothole-avoidance robot

Where
Northeastern University
When
February – June 2025
Role
Two-person project
Stack
PyBullet, PPO, PyTorch, SolidWorks, ToF and IMU

A two-wheel differential-drive robot that senses the terrain and steers around potholes. The hardware was co-designed and built with my project partner. My work focused on sensor and actuator bring-up, real-time motion control, and the avoidance policy developed and evaluated in simulation.

The robot

The robot used a downward-facing sensing setup to observe the terrain ahead while the differential-drive base provided the steering authority. I brought up the sensor interfaces and actuator control, connecting the measurements to the real-time motion-control stack and validating the resulting behaviour on a physical test track.

Training terrain

Potholes were generated procedurally rather than authored, so no two training episodes shared a layout and the policy could not simply memorise a course.

The same procedural generation also made evaluation possible on terrain the policy had not seen during training, rather than testing it on a fixed set of hand-authored layouts.

Policy

The avoidance policy used PPO with a two-branch actor-critic network, fusing a rendered depth observation with vertical IMU acceleration. Evaluated over 300 episodes on unseen terrain, it avoided 87% of potholes.

What was hard

The reward function went through four iterations. The robot first learned to spin in place, which collects no penalty and makes no progress. Adding a penalty for spinning did not fix it, because punishing the symptom does not remove the incentive; the behaviour turned into a slow spiral instead. It only broke once the reward paid for visiting new grid cells, which gave forward motion something to earn.

From simulation to hardware

Simulation made it possible to develop and evaluate the avoidance policy across procedurally generated terrain before running the system on the physical platform. The hardware then introduced the parts that simulation could not capture: real sensor interfaces, actuator response and the timing of the complete sensing-to-control loop.

I brought up the ToF and IMU interfaces, connected their measurements to the motion-control system, and validated the robot's behaviour on a physical test track. The goal was not just to make each component work independently, but to get sensing, decision-making and actuation working together as one real-time system.

Limits

The depth observation was rendered, with no sensor noise, dropouts or dependence on surface reflectivity. The IMU signal was differenced from simulated velocities rather than modelled as a real sensor with bias and drift. Potholes were the only terrain feature, with no slopes, raised obstacles or moving objects. The reward also carried a turn-direction bonus that helped break symmetry during training but biased the policy and would not belong in a serious version.
